RE: ISSUE ONE

AN INTERVIEW WITH THE CURATOR

An insight into the choices behind the musical pairings of Issue One.

In August, we released our debut issue of The Love Letter magazine, Issue One: EPHEMERA. But a month or so before I sent the final files off to print, I sent a draft to a close friend of mine, Munraj (Mun) Kang. Music has been a common theme of our friendship over the years, from sharing an AirPod during lunch break to traipsing across London for a gig, and so I decided to entrust him with the task of creating a playlist for the issue. His only requirement: to assign a single song to each piece in the magazine. After a few weeks, the playlist was complete, and having already pored over the pieces myself, I was eager to press play and listen to what I hoped would be a new perspective on the literature. It’s safe to say I picked the right person for the job.

The intersection of music and literature has always been a point of interest, and the connection this playlist has with Issue One is a remarkable illustration of just that. From the living-room folk songs of Utah Phillips to the rolling synths of Aphex Twin to the hysterical delights of Linda and Paul McCartney’s RAM, the playlist’s outright refusal of homogeneity mirrors the same diversity found in the pieces chosen for our debut issue. Mun considers atmosphere, story, lyrics and so much more, even tying in his own personal reflections on the pieces. The initial task had become much more than just a banal musical analysis; it had become a representation of someone’s relationship with a select few pieces of literature.

- Eva Azazel
INTERVIEWER

When I first sent you the draft and you read it for the first time, did you go in with an idea of what music you thought would work or did you go in with a blank slate?

MUN

I did go in with music in my head. I actually created a playlist called Ephemera Ideas, where I went through about six years of my listening history, just reminding myself of all the different stuff that was out there and what I could possibly use without even seeing the pieces.

I had a collection of about three or four hours’ worth of songs where I thought, these could make it, depending on what the pieces are. Some were love songs, some weren’t love songs, just songs that evoked certain feelings.

Obviously the vast majority didn’t make it. Although some of the ideas were really good, the playlist ended up becoming really song-specific. It was about what the songs themselves evoked. Sometimes I’d read a piece and think, wow, that’s the song, and it was nothing like the pre-planned idea I’d had before reading the text. Every piece was so unique and so different that there wasn’t one universal idea of EPHEMERA, or one universal love song that could define everything. Everything had a sense of individuality, and the differences between the pieces made me think every pairing had to be tailored specifically.

INTERVIEWER

What was your approach?

MUN

Being a spreadsheet guy, the first thing I did was read it front to back. No analysing, just reading. Maybe I’d write down two or three words for each piece - what it’s trying to say, or a couple words to describe it. Then I’d come back after I’d finished and think, what does this make me feel? What songs come to mind straight away? Without doing too much research, I’d just write down whatever songs immediately popped into my head.

For a lot of them I came up with three or four songs. Some pieces I already knew the song and I’d come back to it later. Then it was basically rereading each piece with each song in mind. If I had four songs, I’d reread it four times. How does this one feel? What about this one?

Some pieces were just...hard. Philadelphia Love Song by Kate Hodges, for example, had about eight potential songs. I was really racking my brain with that one.

There was just so much more text. It spans a much longer period than the other pieces, there are more emotions, more characters, more little stories going on. It made the feeling much harder to pinpoint exactly. The boys, the teacher, her father. In the end I decided to take the perspective of the main character looking back on everything. To me it felt wrapped up in memory - a memory of a period of time, of looking back.

INTERVIEWER

And so you chose All the Years by Beach House.

MUN

Yeah. I was thinking it was too much of a task to assign a specific song straight away for that piece, but an artist like Beach House has so many songs where the feeling matched.

Beach House was a big part of my life when I was nineteen or twenty. Their music has this feeling of missing something, reminiscing, this cathartic, gut-wrenching nostalgia. That’s exactly what the piece evoked. So I thought, rather than searching for one song straight away, I’ll just go through the Beach House catalogue.

There were songs where I thought, Oh, I remember really loving this one. I remember one being my favourite Beach House song because it had that same feeling of time passing, but it didn’t quite fit the piece. I knew there were probably other songs in their catalogue that would work better.

So I went through the early Beach House albums, the first three or four, and found it that way.

INTERVIEWER

Were there any other pairings where you started with an artist before a specific song?

MUN

There might have been. The easiest picks I made were actually the prologue and epilogue because Hope Sandoval was a complete no-brainer.

Not because of the lyrics, though. I chose the instrumental from the Bavarian Fruit Bread, Baby Let Me. There’s no singing at all, but I knew it had to be there because of the feeling of the epilogue.

For the prologue it was different. It was the Utah Phillips album you introduced me to, Starlight on the Rails, which had a huge impact on me about a year ago.

The whole EPHEMERA issue made me think about letters. Not memorabilia exactly, but letters between two people that show a fleeting love.

There’s a story that comes before I Think of You on that album, about a past love. The relationship Utah tells us about doesn’t last, and it probably wasn’t even the greatest relationship ever, but for that moment they were each other’s person. It was an escape from the war. It was writing to someone you loved, writing down your true feelings and explaining what you’re living through.

Writing lets you convey things that can’t really be said through conversations, texts or phone calls. I find that in myself as well. Give me a pen and I’ll write things that feel like they’ve come from a second layer of my brain rather than just the surface.

So for that pairing it came down to I Think of You and A Clearing in the Forest, both sung by Rosalie Sorrels. They’re both exceptional songs, but I Think of You just felt like the perfect fit because, at its heart, it’s a letter.

INTERVIEWER

There are a few other songs you chose that don’t have any lyrics. Why? Was it the atmosphere?

MUN

A hundred percent atmosphere.

There weren’t any written-down criteria I had to follow when choosing songs, but atmosphere, feeling, conversation and intention were all things I was considering.

Where something might not pair well with specific lyrics, I’d think instead: What am I feeling just from hearing the sounds? Or, what am I feeling just from reading these words where there’s no sound at all? Can the music take me somewhere, in the same way reading a book can transport you somewhere?

I didn’t want everything to be on the nose, where the lyrics matched exactly what the piece was saying, because that can completely ignore the atmosphere.

For the instrumental pairings, Phone Call - From “Eternal Sunshine of the Spotless Mind” Score for Limerant by Cameron Dale was one where I thought it just hurts.

And then #20 by Aphex Twin for Where the Salt Water Licks Your Wounds by K.D. Harbour. That one gave me this image of lying on a moonlit beach. All you can hear are the waves crashing, and there’s a dead body glistening under the moonlight. I don’t think any song with lyrics could have done that image more justice. There’s nobody else on the beach. You’re just alone with a corpse.

INTERVIEWER

How did you know you’d found the right song?

MUN

Well, there were some where it was instantaneous. No shadow of a doubt. Sun in My Mouth by Björk for The Date by Christine Riggott comes to mind.

Not because I specifically wanted to get a Björk song, or even something from Vespertine, onto the playlist. But as I was reading the piece, I knew immediately it had to be something from Vespertine. It’s Björk at her best.

Vespertine, for me, is love in a way that’s really hard to describe. It’s not just lovey-dovey —it’s real, raw intimacy and sexual attraction, but it’s done so beautifully. Sun in My Mouth is actually one of my least-played songs from the album, so it gave me an excuse to listen to Vespertine all over again.

There’s a sensuality in The Date. It’s two characters so entwined, like two eagles gripping each other’s feet and only letting go at the last second.

Björk does intimacy so well. The way her songs emulate this “WE’RE GONNA FUCK,” but somehow in a way that could bring you to tears.

INTERVIEWER

Svefn-g-englar by Sigur Rós—it’s incredibly atmospheric, and there are slight lyrical connections as well. Talk to me about that pairing for James Barnes’ Voyager.

MUN

When it came to Voyager, there were really only two songs in the running. It was one of those pieces where I knew I’d come back to it, but those two songs were in my head immediately.

Looking back now, I realise the atmosphere played a huge part in almost all my choices, but with this one it completely encapsulated the piece for me.

The other song I considered was Ladies and Gentlemen We Are Floating in Space by Spiritualized. Maybe it was too on the nose. Obviously it’s a space song and a love song, it feels like somebody leaving the Earth’s atmosphere and floating away, which is exactly how the text read to me.

A big part of choosing Svefn-g-englar was this sense of collective humanity. I’ve never understood Icelandic, I’ve never really looked up the lyrics beyond maybe glancing at the translation once, but everything about that song gives me this feeling of worldwide unity. I don’t need to understand the words to feel what it’s trying to say.

INTERVIEWER

Komm, süsser Tod by Arianne from The End of Evangelion for Mnemopsis Ledyei by Valeria E. Slynt. What was it about that song that made you choose it? For me it was the lyrics, but was it the same for you?

MUN

This one was the pairing that excited me the most. Finding it, realising how well it worked with the piece—I was jumping up for joy.

Not only is The End of Evangelion such a monumental part of my life, and our friendship, but the lyrics are incredible.

The story takes a completely different approach. Although I don’t think anyone in the magazine takes a uniform approach to the theme, this one has two pieces running alongside each other. I wouldn’t even say they’re two different perspectives, more two concurrent narratives: the sea walnut and the person on the train.

The protagonist starts thinking about sin and questioning this crying baby. That immediately made me think about original sin - a baby being brought into the world and immediately becoming sinful despite knowing absolutely nothing.

Then, with the sea walnut, everything ties together at the end. The protagonist doesn’t realise he’s being a bit of a shit until he reflects on everything. He starts thinking, maybe I have done bad things, maybe I am an asshole.

And that’s where it tied in perfectly with the lyrics.

I know, I know I’ve let you down
I’ve been a fool to myself

...

I wish that I could turn back time
‘Cause now the guilt is all mine

There’s so much embarrassment in that self-reflection. He regrets things he’s done, he feels like he’s sinned, he wants to be a better person, but time has to keep moving forward and you can’t turn it back.

I feel like if he looked back five years from now, he’d still be doing the same thing—thinking I did this, I did that.

That embarrassment, that guilt on the train, wanting to turn back time... then the ending, where everything comes together with the sea walnut. The sea walnut has survived nuclear bombs, generations, entire eras, and yet it all still comes tumbling down. It’s the Third Impact. It’s this final reflection, this final return.

The sea walnut returns to having no anus, the baby stops crying, the protagonist comes to the conclusion that maybe he isn’t a good person all the time, and maybe nobody is.

It just felt perfect.

Obviously I was giddy about getting this song into the playlist but honestly I don’t think any other song could have done the piece more justice.

INTERVIEWER

Did you ever think about the length of the songs? Svefn-g-englar and Komm, süsser Tod are both nearly ten minutes long.

MUN

I was definitely aware that if you read alongside the music you’d probably either finish the piece way before the song ended or way after.

Originally that worried me because it was another constraint that could take away from the pairing I wanted. But then I thought, I don’t actually think people, especially someone reading the magazine for the first time, should read and listen at the same time.

I’d recommend listening afterwards, because that’s basically the process I went through while making the playlist. I think hearing the song afterwards lets you see the piece in a slightly different light. It’s almost like hearing a complete stranger perform something that has no actual connection to the author or the story, but somehow arrives at the same emotional place.

INTERVIEWER

You paired Moby’s When It’s Cold I’d Like to Die with The Suicide of an Academic Postponed by Becca N. Frankel. That was an interesting choice because, to me at least, the poem starts off reflective and deeply sad, but ends with this hopeful turn. The child gives the narrator a red leaf, which explains the “postponed” part of the title—they realise they want to stay here and try to be happy.

The song you chose doesn’t really have that hopeful ending.

MUN

The song omits the hopeful ending, but it doesn’t erase the feelings the academic might still have.

Yes, the narrator receives the leaf and starts looking at things more positively, but everything they’ve felt beforehand, this terrible pain of being alive, it’s still there, and that’s what’s in the song.

To me, the Moby track feels like someone’s final moments. They’re closing their eyes, and the song slowly fades out as the world turns grey, the colour drains away, and then everything finally goes dark.

So yes, it leaves out that more positive ending, but I wouldn’t say the poem has a happy ending either. It’s more like a pause, or a reminder.

The sadness the academic feels throughout their life is still embedded in that song.

I do feel a little bad about leaving that hopeful note out, but it’s incredibly difficult to capture everything that poem does in one piece of music. Sad songs rarely end with hope.

I still come back to the same belief: the poem is hiding this underlying truth that, yes, there are good days, but the feelings don’t disappear. They’re postponed - they’re not gone forever.

INTERVIEWER

Going back to some of the more difficult pairings, did any of the pieces take longer to pair than you thought they might?

MUN

Nina Lewis’ poem Keeping Spring probably had about eight different songs in the running.

One of them was George Harrison’s All Things Must Pass, but it was just way too on the nose. It fit one aspect of the poem, but it ignored a lot of the other things I wanted to capture.

All Things Must Pass is more like, “Oh no, lots of bad things are happening, but this too shall pass.” Whereas Keeping Spring had a lot more nuance. To me it was saying that, to appreciate the good, the bad has to happen as well. It’s more of an equal trade-off than simply everything gets better. As much as I love George, it wasn’t the right choice.

I also really wanted to use Joanna Newsom’s Sawdust & Diamonds, but when I thought about it properly I realised I wanted Joanna on the playlist more than I wanted that specific pairing. It wasn’t actually the best fit.

In the end I went with Delicious by Linda Perhacs.

Linda captures this feeling of everything seeming really good. Everything’s delicious, but you can still feel that something isn’t quite right, even if, on the surface, everything seems amazing.

The way she sings “It’s the...” specifying rather than generalising... It’s the way someone does something, this one specific thing that makes you feel good, but it’s not everything.

That’s what I got from the poem. The flowers have to go to make way for the harvest. The day has to end to make way for the night. It’s this equal trade-off between good and bad. Not that everything is good or everything is bad, but that life is always a give and take.

INTERVIEWER

Your own experiences seem to come through quite a lot in these choices. Beach House, Komm, süsser Tod… somebody else might have made completely different pairings. I feel these choices are yours because they’re tied to your own life.

MUN

Well, I hope so.

Everything in this playlist is music I’ve picked up over the last six years, and there are songs that are really close to me, like the Moby track and Komm, süsser Tod. I associate those songs with really specific periods of my life.

When It’s Cold I’d Like to Die takes me straight back to the winter of my first year at university, when it was cold, I did want to die, and I was an academic.

Reading that poem while listening to that song took me back to being an academic who was serially unhappy. It resonated because I’d been there.

All the songs came from my own library, but a lot of them resonated because they let me put myself in the writer’s shoes. Or maybe because I’d already been in those shoes myself.

Love Songs on the Radio by Mojave 3 for Dear Susie by Misha Paysinger is another one. To me that song speaks of a love so intense it almost becomes mythological, and I’ve felt that before. The lyrics don’t directly match the piece, but my response to reading it was remembering that feeling of this huge, almost mythical love. So the song I chose was the one I already associated with that feeling.

Even just that line: “She looks just like an angel when she walks across the room...“

Another one I really liked getting onto the playlist was Aga Ujma‘s song Cloud 9, which again isn’t entirely in English.

INTERVIEWER

It comes straight after the Björk track as well, and you can hear some obvious Björk influences.

MUN

Yeah—I couldn’t put two Björk songs on the playlist.

I was lucky enough to see Aga perform live. I walked in, she was sitting there with the harp, and my immediate reaction was just, “Dude... I’m almost crying.”

Reading The Ironing by Penny Nolte, I imagined a child remembering the rituals of their childhood and how everything used to be. The song takes me somewhere completely different. It puts me in a garden with clothes hanging on the washing line. I’m about three feet tall, looking up at everything.

None of that’s actually in the story, but that’s where the piece took me.

INTERVIEWER

What is music to you?

MUN

Music is the greatest thing to ever fucking happen.

I genuinely think it’s one of the biggest things that separates us from every other form of life. Every culture has music. Every period of history has music. Wherever there were people, there were songs, there was rhythm, there was banging on rocks and skins. Music is just deeply human.

Even in this little fourteen-song playlist there’s such a huge range, and it still barely scratches the surface of what’s out there. Every song evokes something different.

There are songs where, as soon as I hear them, I’m transported somewhere. That’s exactly where I was in August 2022, standing outside a bus station. I’ve kept a playlist every month for the last six years. It’s a diary. Instead of writing down what I was feeling, I just saved the songs.
